Perth's growth should work for Perth.

Who is Perth growing for?

I'm Paul Quinnell. I'm 41, I have a young family here, and I'm running for one of five seats on Perth Council.

Perth is planning for 10,500 people by 2054. That is roughly a third more than live here now. Growth of that size is not automatically good or bad. It depends entirely on who pays for it and who it is built for.

That is the question I want on the table before council approves the next thing.

Who I am

I grew up in social housing. I chose to live here.

I grew up in social housing in rural England. My family did not have much, and the difference between a decent childhood and a hard one came down to decisions made by people I never met. Council decisions, mostly. That is not an abstract idea to me.

I came to Canada, and out of everywhere I could have settled, I picked this town. My family lives on Garden Avenue. My kids will grow up here. I am not passing through and I am not building a career on the way to somewhere else.

I am not a developer, a landlord, or a lawyer. I read the by-laws, the budget and the Official Plan because someone should, and because most of what frustrates people about this town is already written down in a document nobody reads.

The question

Growth is coming. The terms are still open.

Perth's Official Plan sets a target of 10,500 residents by 2054, drawn from the County's growth allocation and the 2024 Residential Lands Needs Assessment. The 2024 Official Plan update moved effectively all remaining developable land in town into development designations. The land is designated. The applications are in motion.

What has not been settled is the part that lands on your tax bill. Who pays for the water mains, the road capacity, the stormwater systems and the recreation facilities that a bigger town needs. Whether any of the new housing is priced for the people already living here or only for the people arriving.

Those are council decisions. Not provincial ones, not federal ones. Council sets the development charges. Council approves the zoning. Council passes the budget. The next four years are when those terms get written.

Building it right the first time costs less than fixing it later.

Platform

Four things I would push for.

Short version at the top of each. Tap any of them for the detail and the documents behind it.

01 Homes for people who already live here Sequencing matters. Rules should be written before the approvals they are meant to govern, and affordability should be a real conversation early, not a form filed after the decision.

Perth spent twenty-six years operating under a zoning by-law passed in 2000. The replacement was adopted in August 2026. In the years while it was being written, some of the largest development decisions in the town's recent history were being made under the old rules.

I do not think that was anyone's plan. I think it is what happens when a town takes on a growth target before it updates the framework that governs growth. The order matters, and I would rather council learned that lesson than repeated it.

The Official Plan already contains a pre-consultation policy on affordability at section 8.1.3.9. My view is that it should function as a genuine early conversation about unit mix and price point, held while the design is still changeable, rather than a box ticked once the shape of a project is already set.

There is also a timing issue worth knowing about. Under the Planning Act, once a municipality passes a new comprehensive zoning by-law, privately initiated amendments are restricted for two years unless council specifically allows them. That window is a real opportunity to get the framework right, and it closes.

What this is based on

  • Zoning By-law 3358, in force from 2000 until August 2026.
  • Staff report 2026-COW-5.6, Town of Perth New Zoning By-law discussion draft.
  • Official Plan section 8.1.3.9, affordability pre-consultation.
  • Planning Act restriction on privately initiated amendments following a new comprehensive zoning by-law.
  • OLT-23-000534, settlement for roughly 940 units at 141 Peter Street.
02 Growth should pay its own way Every dollar of growth-related infrastructure that development charges do not cover is a dollar that comes off the tax levy instead. That is an affordability question for people who already live here.

Development charges are the tool municipalities use to make new development pay for the infrastructure it makes necessary. They are not a tax on housing in any meaningful sense. They are a decision about which of two groups pays: the development that creates the demand, or the existing ratepayers who did not.

Perth's development charges by-law is By-law 4839, passed in 2019. The Town's own Strategic Plan, under Growth, item B, set 2023 to 2025 as the window to complete a new development charge study. That is the Town's commitment, in the Town's own document, and it is a fair thing to ask about.

Two other tools sit unused. Community Benefits Charges are available under the Planning Act for buildings of five storeys or more. Perth's current zoning caps height below that threshold in most areas, so the charge would not bite today. I would still want the by-law in place before anyone asks for extra height, rather than after, because the negotiating position is entirely different in each case.

The other is the federal Housing Design Catalogue, which offers pre-approved designs that cut approval time and cost for small builders. Municipalities can sign on as local partners. It costs the Town very little and it helps exactly the kind of small local builder who cannot absorb a long approvals process.

What this is based on

  • Development Charges By-law 4839, passed 2019.
  • Strategic Plan 2023 to 2027, Growth item B: complete new development charge study, 2023 to 2025.
  • Planning Act section 37, Community Benefits Charges, five storey threshold.
  • Canada Housing Design Catalogue, Local Partner program.
  • Canada-Ontario Development Charge Reduction Program, council application approved June 2026.
03 Built for the weather we're getting Climate spending is not an add-on to the capital plan. It is the cheapest version of repairs the town is going to make anyway.

Perth adopted a Climate Change Action Plan in 2017. The Strategic Plan commits the Town to building climate change adaptation in when replacing infrastructure, and to applying a climate lens to asset replacement decisions. Both are listed as ongoing.

Commitments listed as ongoing are the ones most easily quietly dropped in a tight budget year. My interest is narrow and practical: when a culvert, a storm sewer or a road is being replaced anyway, was it sized for the rainfall we are now getting, or for the rainfall we used to get. The incremental cost of the larger pipe at the time of replacement is small. The cost of doing it twice is not.

This is the least ideological item on this list. It is an asset management argument. It happens to also be a climate argument.

What this is based on

  • Town of Perth Climate Change Action Plan, adopted 2017.
  • Strategic Plan 2023 to 2027, Quality of Life item B: build in climate change adaptation when replacing infrastructure, ongoing.
  • Strategic Plan, Quality of Life item B: urban tree cover plan, 2024 to 2027.
  • Official Plan sections 5.3 and 7.3, climate resilient design expectations for development applications.
04 A councillor who actually shows up Not a policy. A commitment about how I would do the job.

I would attend the meetings, read the packages before the meetings, and be at the votes. That should be a low bar and mostly it is. This term it also happens to matter arithmetically.

Ontario's strong mayor powers took effect in Perth on May 1, 2025. Every sitting member of council voted on April 14, 2025 to formally oppose them and to ask to be removed. The Province proceeded anyway. Under those rules the mayor proposes the budget, council can amend it by majority, the mayor can veto, and it takes five votes to override that veto.

Five votes on a seven member council. An absent councillor is not a neutral outcome in that arithmetic. It is a vote for whatever the veto protects.

I would also answer emails. If you write to me and I disagree with you, you will get a reply saying so and explaining why, rather than no reply at all.

What this is based on

  • Council resolution of April 14, 2025 opposing the expansion of strong mayor powers.
  • Ontario strong mayor powers in effect in Perth from May 1, 2025.
  • Municipal Act veto override threshold of two thirds of council.

What council controls

A councillor cannot fix your wait at the hospital.

Municipal campaigns collect promises about things municipalities do not run. It is an easy way to sound responsive and a reliable way to disappoint people later.

Here is the honest split. Everything in my platform sits in the left column. Where something in the right column matters to Perth, and a lot of it does, the real work is advocacy through the County, AMO, ROMA and the Eastern Ontario Wardens' Caucus, plus keeping the tax levy stable enough that people can afford to live here while those fights play out.

Perth Council decides

  • Zoning and planning approvals
  • Development charges and levies
  • The annual budget and tax levy
  • Water and sewer rate structure
  • Roads, sidewalks, parks, arena
  • Building and property standards
  • Community Improvement Plans
  • Disposal of surplus Town land

Perth Council does not decide

  • Hospital funding and doctor supply
  • Schools and school board budgets
  • Affordable housing units, run at the County level
  • Provincial housing and planning law
  • Income supports and social assistance rates
  • Interest rates, trade and immigration
  • The pool building, owned by the school board
  • Police services board funding formula

If I am elected and you ask me for something in the right column, I will tell you it is in the right column, and then tell you who does decide it and what I can usefully do about it. That is worth more than a promise I cannot keep.

How to vote

There are no paper ballots this year.

Perth is voting by internet and telephone only. If you are waiting for a polling station to open near you, it is not coming. The letter from the Town is the thing that matters. It contains the PIN you need to vote, and without it you cannot.

  1. Make sure you are on the voters' list Check and update your registration at registertovoteon.ca. If you have moved, changed your name, or turned 18 recently, do this now.
  2. Watch for your PIN letter in the mail The Town mails it to the address on the voters' list. Do not throw it out. If it has not arrived, contact the Town Clerk's office.
  3. Vote online or by phone between October 19 and October 26 You can vote any time in that window from anywhere. It takes a few minutes.
  4. You have five votes for council Five seats, no wards, everyone in town chooses from the same list. You can use all five. I am asking for one of them.
